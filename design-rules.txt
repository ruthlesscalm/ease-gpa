WEBSITE DESIGN RULES Anti-Generic UI, Visual Quality, Content Integrity,
Accessibility, and Production Standards Version: 1.0

PURPOSE

Use this document as the design and implementation standard for this
project. The goal is to create distinctive, coherent, usable,
production-quality websites that feel deliberately designed for their
specific brand, audience, and purpose—not like generic AI-generated
templates.

These rules are intended to prevent lazy defaults, not to ban useful
design techniques. A pattern listed as something to avoid may still be
used when there is a clear, project-specific reason. Do not add visual
effects or components merely because they are common in AI-generated
websites. Every design decision should support at least one of the
following: content clarity, usability, accessibility, brand identity,
information hierarchy, or a deliberate visual concept.

HOW TO APPLY THESE RULES

1.  Read this entire file before starting UI design or making
    substantial UI changes.
2.  Inspect the existing project, its audience, content, functionality,
    brand direction, and current design system before changing anything.
3.  Preserve established conventions that work well. Do not redesign
    unrelated parts of the project unnecessarily.
4.  Apply the guidelines consistently across all relevant pages,
    components, responsive breakpoints, and interaction states.
5.  Treat explicit project requirements and factual source content as
    authoritative. Never invent business facts to make a design look
    more convincing.
6.  Use judgment. A listed pattern is a warning against unconsidered
    defaults, not an unconditional prohibition.
7.  Before finishing, review the actual implementation against this
    complete document and fix avoidable violations. Do not claim to have
    reviewed or tested anything that you did not actually review or
    test.

  ------------------------------------------------
  1. VISUAL DESIGN, COLOR, SURFACES, AND EFFECTS
  ------------------------------------------------

1.1 HARSH GRADIENTS Avoid harsh, high-contrast, oversaturated, or
arbitrary gradients used as a shortcut to make a page look modern. Do
not automatically place a bright gradient behind every hero, heading,
button, or card. Prefer a considered color palette, controlled contrast,
and purposeful surfaces. Use gradients when they support the brand or
art direction, and keep transitions intentional and visually coherent.

1.2 LUCIDE ICONS Do not automatically add Lucide icons to every heading,
feature, navigation item, button, card, or list. Icons should improve
recognition, scanning, or interaction clarity. Use a consistent icon
family and stroke weight when icons are appropriate. Avoid mixing
unrelated icon styles, using icons as decoration without purpose, or
substituting an icon for a label when the meaning would become unclear.
Text-only UI is often better.

1.3 PURE WHITE BACKGROUNDS Do not default every page and section to a
pure white (#FFFFFF) background. Establish a deliberate surface system
that suits the brand and content. Consider subtle neutral backgrounds,
differentiated section surfaces, borders, and contrast where they
improve hierarchy. Pure white is still appropriate when it is
intentional, accessible, and visually effective; do not add tinted
backgrounds just for novelty.

1.4 RAINBOW COLORING Avoid using many unrelated accent colors, rainbow
gradients, or a different bright color for every card or section without
a coherent system. Define a limited, purposeful palette with primary,
secondary, accent, background, surface, border, and semantic colors as
needed. Use color consistently to communicate hierarchy or state. Do not
rely on color alone to convey meaning.

1.5 DROP SHADOWS Avoid excessive, dark, blurry, or stacked drop shadows.
Do not give every card, button, section, and container a floating
appearance. Use spacing, contrast, borders, and surface changes before
adding elevation. Shadows should communicate real layering or
interaction and should be subtle and consistent. Strong shadows are
acceptable only when they are part of a deliberate visual direction.

1.6 PURPLE AND BLACK AS A DEFAULT Do not automatically choose
purple-on-black, dark backgrounds with neon accents, or the familiar
generic AI/SaaS palette. Select colors based on the project’s identity,
audience, content, industry, and intended emotional tone. A
purple-and-black palette is acceptable when it genuinely suits the
brand; it must not be the unexamined default.

1.7 LIQUID GLASS Do not apply glassmorphism, translucent panels,
backdrop blur, glossy surfaces, or liquid-glass effects everywhere just
because they look fashionable. These effects can reduce legibility,
increase visual noise, and create unnecessary rendering cost. Use them
only when the design concept calls for layered transparency. Verify
contrast, text readability, performance, and behavior across supported
browsers.

1.8 NEON COLORS Avoid unnecessary neon accents, glowing borders,
fluorescent gradients, and intense glow effects. Do not use them to make
ordinary buttons, cards, or headings appear more exciting. Use a
restrained palette and ensure interactive elements remain easy to
identify. Neon styling is appropriate only when it supports a deliberate
brand or product concept.

1.9 BASIC PASTEL COLORS Do not default to generic pastel blue, lavender,
pink, or mint merely because they make a page appear soft and friendly.
Select colors that support the brand and content. Pastels can be useful,
but establish enough contrast and variation to maintain hierarchy and
readability. Avoid a palette that feels interchangeable with hundreds of
other templates.

1.10 RADIAL ORBS Do not add large blurred radial-gradient circles,
floating glowing blobs, or decorative orbs to fill empty hero areas by
default. Empty space is not automatically a problem. Use real imagery,
meaningful product visuals, typography, composition, or whitespace
instead. Add abstract orbs only when they are a deliberate part of the
art direction and do not compete with content.

1.11 DOT GRIDS Do not add dot grids, graph-paper backgrounds,
crosshairs, or repeated technical patterns merely to make a page feel
like a startup landing page. Decorative patterns must have a clear
relationship to the brand or content. Keep backgrounds quiet enough that
text and controls remain the primary focus.

1.12 COLORED LEFT STRIPES Avoid adding a bright vertical stripe to the
left edge of every card, list item, callout, or section. Do not use a
colored stripe as a substitute for real hierarchy. Use it only when it
communicates category, status, grouping, or a purposeful visual motif.
Apply such accents consistently if they are part of the design system.

  -------------------------------------
  2. LAYOUT, COMPOSITION, AND SPACING
  -------------------------------------

2.1 THREE FEATURE CARDS IN EVERY ROW Do not automatically arrange every
set of features into three equal cards. Choose a layout based on the
content’s actual relationships and importance. Consider editorial
sections, lists, split layouts, comparison tables, asymmetric
compositions, image-and-text sections, or fewer, more substantial cards.
Three columns are appropriate when the content genuinely supports them;
they should not be the default for every section.

2.2 BENTO GRIDS Do not use bento grids for every feature section or as a
universal way to make a website look contemporary. A bento layout should
express a meaningful hierarchy between different types or levels of
content. Avoid arbitrary card sizes, excessive nested boxes, and grids
that make simple information harder to scan. Use a straightforward
layout when it communicates better.

2.3 REPETITIVE SECTION STRUCTURE Avoid making every section follow the
same formula: centered heading, short subtitle, three cards, and a
call-to-action button. Vary composition only when it improves the story
and hierarchy—not for variety alone. Give each section a layout that
fits its purpose while preserving consistent typography, spacing,
colors, and component behavior.

2.4 WHITESPACE Use whitespace intentionally. Do not fill every empty
area with cards, illustrations, decorative shapes, badges, icons, or
floating elements. Equally, do not leave accidental gaps that make the
page feel unfinished or break the reading flow. Whitespace should
establish grouping, focus, pacing, and visual breathing room.

2.5 EM DASHES Avoid overusing em dashes (—) in headings, marketing copy,
labels, and explanatory text, especially when they make generated copy
feel repetitive. Use normal punctuation and sentence structure when
clearer. An em dash is not prohibited; use it naturally when it improves
the writing.

2.6 TERMINAL WINDOWS Do not add fake terminal windows, command-line
panels, code snippets, blinking cursors, or developer-console aesthetics
unless the product, audience, or brand genuinely benefits from them.
These elements are not generic decoration for every technology company.
If a terminal is included as a real product example, make the content
credible, readable, and relevant.

2.7 GENERIC HERO SECTIONS Avoid defaulting to the same hero formula:
oversized headline, gradient background, two pill-shaped buttons,
floating cards, and abstract decorative shapes. First determine the
audience, page purpose, main message, and primary action. Choose a hero
composition that communicates these clearly. Depending on the project,
the right solution may be an editorial layout, full-width photograph,
product interface, illustration, typographic treatment, concise
introduction, or another relevant composition.

2.8 RESPONSIVE COMPOSITION Do not treat mobile as a shrunken desktop
layout. Plan how content hierarchy, navigation, images, columns, tables,
and actions should adapt to small screens. Avoid horizontal overflow,
cramped card grids, tiny tap targets, and unnecessary content
duplication. Make deliberate responsive decisions at supported
breakpoints.

2.9 SPACING AND ALIGNMENT Establish a consistent spacing scale and align
related elements intentionally. Avoid arbitrary padding and margins
chosen independently for every component. Use spacing to communicate
relationships: related content should feel grouped, and distinct
sections should have clear separation. Check vertical rhythm across the
full page, not only within individual cards.

  ---------------------------
  3. TYPOGRAPHY AND WRITING
  ---------------------------

3.1 FONT DEFAULTS Do not automatically use Inter, Geist, or Space
Grotesk on every project. These are valid fonts, not mandatory defaults.
Choose typography according to brand character, audience, readability,
available font files, language support, and performance. Use a coherent
type system with clear roles for display headings, section headings,
body copy, labels, and metadata.

3.2 TYPOGRAPHIC HIERARCHY Define a deliberate type scale, font weights,
line heights, letter spacing, and text widths. Do not make every heading
oversized or every label tiny and low-contrast. Keep body text
comfortable to read, avoid excessively long line lengths, and make
heading hierarchy clear. Check actual content rather than judging
typography only with placeholder text.

3.3 GENERIC AI MARKETING COPY Avoid vague, interchangeable phrases such
as “revolutionize your workflow,” “unlock your potential,”
“next-generation solutions,” or “seamless experience” unless they are
accurate and specifically justified. Write for the actual audience and
page purpose. Prefer concrete explanations of what the product, service,
institution, or organization does and why it matters.

3.4 FAKE TESTIMONIALS Never fabricate testimonials, customer quotations,
reviews, star ratings, named customers, client logos, case studies,
awards, or endorsements. Do not create fake social proof to make a page
feel populated. If verified material is unavailable, omit the section,
use clearly labelled placeholders during development, or replace it with
factual information that is actually available.

3.5 FAKE METRICS AND CLAIMS Never invent numbers such as customer
counts, placement rates, conversion improvements, years of experience,
satisfaction percentages, rankings, revenue, performance gains, or other
business claims. Do not infer that a statistic is true because it is
plausible. Use verified source material or clearly mark content as a
placeholder that must be replaced before production.

3.6 “IT’S NOT X, IT’S Y” Do not repeatedly use the copywriting formula
“It’s not X, it’s Y” (including variations such as “not just X, but Y”)
as a default way to create dramatic headings. Use direct, specific
language. The formula may be used occasionally when it genuinely
expresses an important distinction, but it should not become a
repetitive pattern across a page.

3.7 CONTENT DENSITY AND CLARITY Keep content as concise as the task
allows without removing information users need. Use headings,
paragraphs, lists, tables, and captions according to the content. Do not
split every sentence into a separate card or label. Avoid overly dense
text blocks, but do not reduce meaningful information to vague slogans.

3.8 REAL CONTENT Use supplied names, copy, prices, program details,
policies, contact information, and claims accurately. Do not invent
university departments, course offerings, admission dates, product
capabilities, prices, contact details, or business facts. If content is
missing, ask for it when necessary or use an unmistakable placeholder in
development. Do not present placeholders as real information.

  -----------------------------------------------------
  4. COMPONENTS, ICONS, LISTS, AND COMMON UI PATTERNS
  -----------------------------------------------------

4.1 CHECKMARK BULLETS Do not use checkmarks for every feature list,
benefit, requirement, or paragraph. Choose a presentation appropriate to
the meaning: standard bullets for unordered information, numbered lists
for sequences, checkboxes for actionable tasks, and checkmarks for
genuine completion or verified benefits. Avoid using decorative
checkmarks to imply claims are verified when they are not.

4.2 THREE PRICING TIERS Do not automatically create three pricing tiers
or force an offering into a Basic/Pro/Enterprise structure. Pricing must
reflect the actual business model and available offerings. Use the
number of plans that genuinely exist, explain meaningful differences,
and do not invent prices or features. If pricing is not available,
present an honest contact or enquiry path rather than fabricated
packages.

4.3 FAKE PRODUCT DEMOS Do not create misleading product dashboards, fake
app screens, simulated data, fake analytics, or interface previews that
imply capabilities the product does not have. Product mockups must
reflect real or explicitly illustrative functionality. Clearly label
demonstration data when necessary, and ensure visual examples support
the actual product story.

4.4 EMOJIS Do not use emojis as default decorations in professional
websites, headings, feature cards, navigation, or buttons. Use them only
when they fit the brand and audience and remain clear across platforms.
Prefer a consistent icon system when a visual symbol is genuinely
helpful; plain text is often the clearest choice.

4.5 ICON OVERUSE Do not put an icon beside every heading, label, button,
paragraph, and card. Too many icons create visual noise and reduce their
usefulness. Add an icon only when it helps users recognize an action,
identify a category, or scan information. Keep icon size, stroke,
alignment, and visual weight consistent.

4.6 COMPONENT NECESSITY Do not add badges, floating widgets,
announcement bars, counters, carousels, accordions, tabs, tooltips, or
other components simply because they are available. Use a component only
when it solves a real content or interaction need. Prefer the simplest
component that communicates the information correctly.

4.7 CONSISTENT CORNER RADII Use a small, coherent set of corner-radius
tokens. Do not give every element a different radius, and do not make
every component excessively rounded or pill-shaped. The radius should
fit the brand, component type, and overall visual language. Keep related
elements consistent.

4.8 NO SKELETON LOADERS WITHOUT A NEED Do not add skeleton loaders to
content that is immediately available, static, or rendered locally
without a meaningful wait. Loading states should represent real
asynchronous work or a genuine delay. When loading indicators are
needed, ensure they match the final content layout, do not flash
unnecessarily, and do not replace appropriate error, empty, or progress
states.

4.9 SPARKLE ICONS Do not automatically add sparkle icons to AI features,
premium features, headings, or buttons. Avoid using sparkles as a
generic signal that something is intelligent, innovative, or high
quality. Use them only when they are part of a deliberate visual
language and do not obscure the feature’s actual purpose.

4.10 ANIMATED ARROWS Do not animate arrows on every link, button, card,
or call to action. Motion should communicate direction, reveal a state
change, or reinforce a meaningful interaction. Keep movement subtle,
consistent, and non-distracting. A static arrow or no arrow is often
sufficient.

4.11 BUTTONS AND ACTIONS Make button labels specific and meaningful. Do
not style every action as the same large, brightly colored pill.
Establish primary, secondary, tertiary, destructive, and text-link
treatments where appropriate. Ensure buttons have real functionality,
clear focus states, adequate target sizes, and visually distinguishable
disabled and loading states.

4.12 PRICING, COMPARISON, AND DATA COMPONENTS Use tables, lists, cards,
and comparison layouts according to the information users need to
compare. Do not turn every piece of data into a card. Ensure prices,
units, limitations, and plan differences are clear and accurate. Do not
hide important conditions in low-contrast footnotes or ambiguous labels.

  --------------------------------------
  5. ANIMATION, HOVER, AND INTERACTION
  --------------------------------------

5.1 HOVER ANIMATIONS Use hover effects to communicate interactivity,
feedback, or hierarchy—not as decoration on every element. Avoid
applying the same lift, scale, glow, and shadow transition to all cards
and buttons. Use motion that fits the component and does not make the
page feel restless. Ensure touch devices do not depend on hover to
expose essential content.

5.2 NEON GLOWS AND ANIMATED GRADIENTS Avoid neon glows, pulsing borders,
constantly moving gradients, and looping visual effects unless they are
central to a deliberate concept. Do not animate the entire page to make
it appear more sophisticated. Check readability, performance,
distraction, and reduced-motion preferences.

5.3 TRANSITION QUALITY Keep transitions purposeful, consistent, and
reasonably short. Animate properties that are appropriate for smooth
rendering. Avoid large movements, excessive bounce, unexpected layout
shifts, and interactions that slow users down. Do not make basic
navigation or important actions wait for decorative animations.

5.4 ACCESSIBLE MOTION Respect the user’s prefers-reduced-motion setting.
Provide reduced or disabled animation where appropriate. Do not rely
exclusively on animation to communicate status, meaning, or essential
information. Avoid flashing or rapidly changing content.

5.5 FUNCTIONAL INTERACTIONS Every visible interactive control must work
as expected. Links should navigate to meaningful destinations; buttons
should perform the action their labels promise; forms should validate
inputs and provide useful feedback; tabs and accordions should have
correct behavior; and menus should open and close accessibly. Do not use
decorative controls that look functional but do nothing.

  -----------------------------------------------------------------
  6. TRUST, LEGAL REQUIREMENTS, ACCESSIBILITY, AND RESPONSIVENESS
  -----------------------------------------------------------------

6.1 TERMS OF SERVICE Do not omit terms of service when they are
appropriate or required for the product, service, or business. Do not
add a meaningless generic terms page simply to check a box. Use
accurate, project-specific legal content and obtain appropriate review
when needed. Never imply that placeholder legal text is complete or
legally approved.

6.2 PRIVACY POLICY Do not omit a privacy policy when applicable. It
should accurately describe the site’s real data collection, use,
storage, sharing, cookies, analytics, forms, and relevant third-party
services. Do not claim that the site collects no data if it uses
analytics, embedded services, or form submissions that collect data.
Privacy disclosures must match the implementation.

6.3 SEMANTIC HTML Use semantic HTML elements appropriately: headings in
a logical hierarchy, buttons for actions, links for navigation, labels
for form controls, and landmarks such as header, nav, main, and footer
when appropriate. Avoid using generic div elements for everything when a
semantic element better describes the purpose. Ensure the document
structure remains understandable without visual styling.

6.4 KEYBOARD ACCESSIBILITY All interactive functionality must be usable
with a keyboard. Provide a visible focus indicator, sensible focus
order, appropriate focus management for dialogs and menus, and
keyboard-operable controls. Do not remove browser focus outlines unless
replacing them with an equally visible and accessible treatment. Avoid
keyboard traps.

6.5 COLOR CONTRAST Maintain sufficient contrast for text, icons,
controls, borders, and focus indicators. Do not use low-contrast gray
text merely to make the design look minimal. Do not rely on color alone
to communicate errors, success, selection, or status. Check contrast in
all relevant themes and interaction states.

6.6 FONT SIZE AND READABILITY Use readable font sizes and line heights.
Avoid tiny captions for important information, overly long lines,
excessive all-caps text, and light font weights on low-contrast
backgrounds. Important controls and labels must remain legible on mobile
devices and at browser zoom.

6.7 FORMS AND ERROR STATES Forms should have explicit labels,
understandable instructions, appropriate input types, accessible
validation, and useful success and error feedback. Preserve user-entered
data when practical after validation errors. Do not communicate errors
through color alone or rely only on placeholder text as a label.

6.8 RESPONSIVE TESTING Check the design at mobile, tablet, laptop, and
wide desktop sizes relevant to the project. Look for clipped text,
overflow, broken grids, awkward image crops, overlapping elements,
navigation failures, and excessively long pages caused by poor spacing.
Do not assume a layout is responsive just because it uses CSS grid or
flexbox.

6.9 REAL CONTENT LENGTHS Test with realistic text lengths, image
proportions, and data volumes. Components must handle long titles,
missing optional fields, empty lists, unusually long names,
localization, and different screen sizes gracefully. Avoid fixed heights
that cut off content. Use truncation only when it is appropriate and
important information remains available.

6.10 LEGAL AND TRUST CONTENT Do not remove contact details, admissions
information, pricing conditions, disclaimers, accessibility information,
privacy disclosures, or other important content merely because they make
the layout less minimal. Present necessary information clearly and in
the right context. Aesthetic simplicity must not come at the expense of
transparency or trust.

  ----------------------------------------------
  7. LOADING, EMPTY, SUCCESS, AND ERROR STATES
  ----------------------------------------------

7.1 REAL LOADING STATES Use a loading indicator only when a user is
waiting for meaningful work to complete. Choose a spinner, progress
indicator, skeleton, or status message based on the task. Do not use
skeleton screens for static content or add artificial delay just to make
the UI look active.

7.2 EMPTY STATES Design useful empty states for genuinely empty data
views. Explain what the user is seeing and, where appropriate, provide a
next action. Do not show fabricated records merely to make a dashboard
look populated.

7.3 ERROR STATES Errors should explain what happened in language users
can understand and, where possible, how to recover. Do not silently
fail, show raw stack traces to ordinary users, or display success
messages when an operation failed. Keep technical details available for
appropriate diagnostics without exposing secrets.

7.4 SUCCESS AND PROGRESS STATES Confirm important successful actions
clearly. For longer operations, communicate progress where reliable
progress information is available. Do not fabricate progress percentages
or display success before the operation has actually completed.

  ------------------------------------------
  8. BRAND FIT AND PROJECT-SPECIFIC DESIGN
  ------------------------------------------

8.1 DESIGN FOR THE ACTUAL AUDIENCE Understand who the website serves and
what users need to accomplish. A student-facing college website, a
developer tool, a portfolio, a retail store, and an enterprise dashboard
should not all look the same. Navigation, content hierarchy, visual
tone, imagery, and actions must fit the actual audience.

8.2 BRAND IDENTITY Choose typography, color, imagery, shapes, and motion
as a coherent system. Do not select a trendy style first and force the
project into it. If the project has an existing brand, respect it while
improving consistency and usability.

8.3 AUTHENTIC IMAGERY Use relevant, high-quality images that support the
content. Avoid unrelated stock photography, generic abstract 3D art,
fabricated product screenshots, and random illustrations added solely to
fill space. Ensure images have appropriate aspect ratios, responsive
behavior, and alternative text where meaningful.

8.4 NO TEMPLATE-LEVEL THINKING Do not treat a familiar landing-page
template as the answer to every project. Start from the content, user
goals, brand, and information structure. A design can be simple and
still be distinctive through composition, typography, authentic content,
spacing, and detail.

8.5 CONSISTENCY WITHOUT MONOTONY Maintain consistent design tokens and
component behavior while allowing sections to have layouts suited to
their purpose. Consistency does not mean every section must look
identical; variety does not mean every section should use a different
style.

  ---------------------------------------
  9. IMPLEMENTATION AND MAINTAINABILITY
  ---------------------------------------

9.1 DESIGN TOKENS Where appropriate, define reusable tokens for colors,
spacing, typography, radii, shadows, breakpoints, and transitions. Avoid
hardcoding unrelated values throughout the application when a shared
token would improve consistency. Do not create an unnecessarily complex
design system for a very small project.

9.2 REUSABLE COMPONENTS Build reusable components when they represent
genuinely repeated patterns. Do not force unrelated sections into one
overly generic component with dozens of conditionals. Components should
have clear responsibilities and support the real content and states they
need to render.

9.3 PERFORMANCE Avoid unnecessary animation, oversized assets, excessive
blur filters, duplicate dependencies, and client-side code that does not
provide a meaningful benefit. Optimize images and load expensive
resources responsibly. Visual polish should not make the website slow or
unresponsive.

9.4 PRESERVE FUNCTIONALITY When improving design, do not accidentally
break routes, form submissions, data fetching, authentication, state
management, SEO metadata, or existing features. Inspect the relevant
implementation before changing it and test affected flows after making
changes.

9.5 HONEST IMPLEMENTATION Do not create controls that appear to work but
are wired to no action. Do not use fake data as if it were real. If a
backend integration is unavailable, clearly identify the limitation and
implement an honest placeholder only when appropriate.

  -----------------------------------
  10. FINAL DESIGN REVIEW CHECKLIST
  -----------------------------------

Before declaring a page or UI task complete, review every relevant item
below:

VISUAL SYSTEM [ ] Gradients, shadows, glass effects, neon, orbs, dots,
and other decorative effects are purposeful rather than automatic. [ ]
The palette is coherent and appropriate to the brand; it does not use
arbitrary rainbow colors or default purple-and-black styling. [ ]
Backgrounds and surfaces establish hierarchy without relying on pure
white everywhere. [ ] Corner radii, borders, shadows, icon styles,
spacing, and typography are consistent. [ ] Decorative stripes,
sparkles, and icons have a meaningful purpose.

LAYOUT [ ] The page does not automatically force every feature into
three cards or every section into a bento grid. [ ] Sections use
compositions suited to their content rather than repeating one generic
pattern. [ ] The hero explains the actual page purpose and does not
blindly use the standard gradient-plus-buttons formula. [ ] Whitespace
is intentional and the reading flow is clear. [ ] The page works at
relevant mobile, tablet, and desktop widths.

CONTENT [ ] Copy is specific, readable, and appropriate for the actual
audience. [ ] There are no fabricated testimonials, logos, metrics,
reviews, awards, pricing, product demos, or claims. [ ] The “It’s not X,
it’s Y” formula, em dashes, checkmark lists, and generic AI marketing
phrases are not overused. [ ] Real names, prices, details, and supplied
claims are preserved accurately. [ ] Missing information is handled
honestly rather than invented.

COMPONENTS AND INTERACTION [ ] Pricing tiers and card layouts reflect
the actual content or offering. [ ] Emojis, Lucide icons, terminal
windows, animated arrows, hover effects, and sparkle icons are used only
when justified. [ ] Skeleton loaders appear only when appropriate. [ ]
Every visible link, button, form, tab, menu, and interactive control
behaves correctly. [ ] Loading, empty, error, success, and disabled
states are handled where necessary.

ACCESSIBILITY AND TRUST [ ] Semantic HTML and a logical heading
hierarchy are used. [ ] Keyboard navigation and visible focus states
work. [ ] Text and controls have adequate contrast and readable sizes. [
] Reduced-motion preferences are respected. [ ] Forms have accessible
labels and understandable validation. [ ] Relevant privacy disclosures,
terms, contact information, and other required trust content are not
omitted. [ ] Legal and privacy content reflects actual site behavior.

ENGINEERING QUALITY [ ] Existing functionality has been preserved. [ ]
Components and design tokens are maintainable and not needlessly
complicated. [ ] Images and effects do not cause avoidable performance
problems. [ ] Realistic content lengths do not cause clipping, overflow,
or layout breakage. [ ] The relevant changes have actually been
inspected and tested.

FINAL PRINCIPLE

Do not confuse visual complexity with quality. The goal is not to ban
every trend, color, icon, card, animation, or gradient. The goal is to
avoid unthinking defaults and build an interface whose decisions are
justified by its audience, content, brand, usability, accessibility, and
purpose.

When uncertain, prefer clarity over decoration, authentic content over
invented content, coherent systems over random styling, and purposeful
simplicity over unnecessary complexity.
